## How AI search actually works

The fundamental difference between traditional search and AI search is one word: synthesis.

Traditional search ranks documents. Google's algorithm evaluates pages against a query, scores them on hundreds of signals, and presents a list. The user picks one. The currency is *ranking position*. You want to be in the top three results because that's where the clicks are.

AI search synthesizes answers. The model — ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, whatever — takes a query, retrieves relevant documents from its training data or live web index, and generates a single coherent answer that names specific entities. The currency is *being named in the answer*. You don't want to rank tenth — you want to be the firm the AI explicitly cites.

That difference cascades. The signals that get you ranked on Google are mostly different from the signals that get you cited by an AI. There's overlap — both reward authoritative content, strong technical foundations, and a coherent entity footprint — but the specific weights differ enough that you can rank #1 on Google and be invisible in ChatGPT, or vice versa.

The four AI surfaces that matter most for legal and medical practices in 2024:

**ChatGPT (with web search)** — Uses Bing-powered retrieval. When a user asks for local providers, ChatGPT pulls from Bing's index plus its training data. Firms that appear in Bing's results and have strong entity signals across the web are more likely to be cited. ChatGPT cites a relatively small number of sources per answer — usually three to five.

**Perplexity** — Multi-source retrieval with inline citations. Perplexity will cite five to ten sources per answer, with footnote-style links. Perplexity weights authority publications, legal directories, and authoritative niche sites heavily. Being cited on a state bar publication or a major legal directory is worth more than being cited on a generic blog.

**Google AI Overviews** — Google's own retrieval layer, generated by Gemini. Overviews are the most aggressive new surface — they appear above the traditional results and answer the query directly. The Overview will often name specific firms. Google weights its existing search signals heavily for Overviews, plus entity data and schema markup.

**Claude (with web search)** — Anthropic's retrieval layer. Cites fewer sources, weights authority signals heavily. Less consumer-facing than ChatGPT but increasingly used by professionals doing research.

Each of these surfaces has its own retrieval logic, its own training data cutoff, its own preference for citation patterns. But all of them reward the same fundamentals: clear entity signals, deep authoritative content, citations from sources the surface trusts, and a structured information architecture that makes it easy to extract and quote your content.

## Why most law firms are invisible to AI search

Most law firms are invisible in AI answers for five very specific reasons. Each is fixable, but none gets fixed by accident.

**1. Thin content.** Most firm websites are bio pages plus a contact form. Practice area pages run 200 to 400 words. There's no depth, no authoritative coverage of practice-area subtopics, no substantive content that an AI could actually quote from. The firm that publishes a 3,000-word authoritative article on Florida probate law gets quoted when someone asks ChatGPT to explain Florida probate. The firm with three paragraphs under *Probate Services* does not.

**2. Poor schema markup.** AI retrieval leans heavily on structured data. LegalService, Attorney, FAQPage, MedicalBusiness, Physician — these schemas tell the model who you are, what you do, where you are, what your credentials are, and how you connect to other entities. Most firm websites have basic Organization schema and nothing else. AI surfaces skip them because they can't disambiguate cleanly.

**3. Citation poverty.** AI models weight authority sites heavily. For legal firms, those are Avvo, Justia, FindLaw, state bar publications, Martindale, Super Lawyers, and a handful of others. For medical practices, they're Healthgrades, Vitals, RealSelf, Zocdoc, and specialty-specific directories. Firms that don't have strong, consistent profiles on these sites don't appear in the training data the AI is drawing from. Citation poverty is the single biggest reason small and mid-sized firms get skipped.

**4. Generic entity signals.** When you ask ChatGPT *who's the top probate attorney in Tampa*, the AI needs to disambiguate. If your firm's website refers to itself by three different names (legal name, doing-business-as, marketing brand), if your Google Business Profile has different category designations than your schema, if your attorneys are referenced inconsistently across the web — the model gets confused and skips you in favor of a firm with cleaner signals.

**5. No published thinking.** AI surfaces weight authors and authoritative voices. When a firm publishes substantive content under an attorney's name — with consistent biographical metadata, schema Person markup, and cross-references to that attorney's other work — the AI starts treating that attorney as an authority on the topic. Firms that publish under a generic "Firm Blog" byline without author authority lose this signal entirely. The first decision most firms make wrong is publishing under "the firm" instead of under specific attorneys with credentials.

You can fix each of these. None of them require a six-figure budget. But fixing them in the right order, at the right pace, with the right operational discipline, is the work — and the firms that do it now compound for years.

## What optimizing for AI search actually looks like

I'll be specific. Six concrete practices, in order of priority.

**Entity disambiguation.** Start with schema markup. LegalService for the firm, Attorney for each lawyer, FAQPage for question-shaped content, BreadcrumbList for navigation. Use sameAs links to authority sources (the firm's Avvo profile, the attorney's state bar listing, the firm's LinkedIn). The schema's job is to tell the AI exactly who you are, where you are, and what you do — with no ambiguity. We typically rebuild a firm's full schema in the first two to three weeks of an engagement. It's foundational. Nothing else compounds until the entity is clean.

**Authoritative content depth.** Pick your top fifteen practice-area topics and publish a substantive article on each — 1,500 to 3,000 words, written for the reader (not for search engines), with clear structure, defined terms, and specific examples. A Tampa probate firm should have authoritative articles on Florida probate timeline, Florida intestate succession, summary administration vs. formal administration, Florida elective share, and so on. Each article is an entity signal: it tells the AI that you understand this topic deeply enough to be cited as an authority.

**Citation-friendly content structure.** AI models extract content in chunks. Long paragraphs without structure get skipped. Q&A patterns get cited. Lists with parallel structure get cited. Defined terms with clear definitions get cited. Summary callouts and TL;DRs get cited. We rewrite content for citation extractability — clear H2 and H3 hierarchy, short sentences inside long paragraphs, defined terms in bold, summary boxes at the top of long sections.

**Authority signals beyond your site.** Get cited where AI surfaces look. For law firms, that means consistent, complete profiles on Avvo, Justia, FindLaw, the state bar's lawyer referral service, and specialty-specific directories (American Bar Association sections, state-specific specialty bars). For medical practices, Healthgrades, Vitals, RealSelf, Zocdoc, and specialty boards. Beyond directories, target a handful of authority publications — legal trade press, medical industry pubs, local business journals, relevant podcasts. Each authoritative mention strengthens your entity in the AI's view.

**Q&A content patterns.** AI loves question-shaped content. *What is a probate? Do I need a divorce attorney or a mediator? How long does an estate take to settle in Florida? When should I see a dermatologist about a mole?* These are the queries clients actually ask. The firms that publish substantive Q&A content — not thin FAQ pages, but full articles structured around real client questions — get cited heavily. FAQ schema markup amplifies this. We typically build a portfolio of fifty to a hundred Q&A pieces for an engaged firm over the first year.

**Author authority.** Stop publishing under "the firm." Start publishing under specific attorneys, with credentials, schema Person markup, consistent biographical signals, and cross-references. When the same attorney byline appears across thirty substantive articles, with a clean entity profile, AI surfaces start treating that attorney as an authority. That attorney gets cited. The firm gets cited.

Each of these is operational, not magical. Each one is something a firm can start this quarter.

Here's what I think happens between now and the end of 2027.

**2024–2025.** Early adopters dominate. Roughly 5% of law firms and medical practices are doing AI search optimization seriously. The work compounds quickly because the field is uncrowded — being one of three cited firms in a metro is dramatically easier in 2024 than it will be in 2026. **2025–2026.** Mainstream awareness arrives. Marketing trade press starts covering AI search as a category. CMOs at mid-market firms ask their agencies what they're doing about it. AI search referrals start appearing in analytics with enough volume that the firms ignoring it can see the gap. Mid-market panic begins. Agencies that resisted the shift scramble to build capability. The firms that started in 2024 are now defending position one against a flood of late-arriving competitors. Many lose ground because they didn't operationalize maintenance.

**2026–2027.** AI search becomes the dominant discovery channel for many legal and medical queries. The 47% Google AI Overviews trigger rate of 2024 climbs past 70% for high-consideration queries. ChatGPT and Perplexity referrals are tracked as primary acquisition channels at any serious firm. The firms that started in 2024 and stayed disciplined are the cited authorities in their markets. The firms that started in 2026 are eighteen months behind and will be in catch-up mode for years.

This isn't speculation. It's the same curve every prior search shift followed. Mobile took five years to become majority share, three years from there to dominate. Voice search compressed the curve. Featured snippets compressed it again. AI search is moving faster than any of them because it's not a behavioral shift — users are still doing the same thing — they're just doing it on a different surface.
