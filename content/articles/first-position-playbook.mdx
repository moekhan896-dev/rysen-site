Most marketing agencies don't have a documented framework. They have a menu of tactics. They offer SEO, paid search, content, social, email, web design — and they pick which ones to run for each client based on what the client asks for, what's selling that quarter, and what the account team is comfortable with. Tactics without a framework produce tactics-shaped results: random, unrepeatable, hard to maintain, hard to defend, hard to scale.

The First Position Playbook is the framework Rysen runs every engagement through. It's not a magic system. It's not a proprietary technology stack. It's a documented operational discipline that determines which tactics we use, which we don't, how we sequence the work, how we report on it, and which clients we take on. The five pillars are explicit because explicit frameworks compound when you actually follow them. Vague frameworks don't.

I'm going to walk through all five pillars. Each one is independently useful. Together, they're the operating model that produces every result we report. The point of writing this down isn't to claim the framework is unique — most of these principles are knowable, and any agency that wanted to could adopt them. The point is to make it explicit so that prospective clients can hold us to it, current clients can see the structure behind the work, and other agencies can see what the bar looks like.

## Pillar 1 — Hyperlocal Dominance

We don't compete statewide. We don't compete regionally. We compete neighborhood-by-neighborhood, metro-by-metro. Your firm doesn't need to rank for *lawyer in Florida*. Your firm needs to rank for *probate lawyer Tampa*, *estate planning Hyde Park*, *divorce attorney Westshore*.

The case for hyperlocal is simple math. Local intent dominates legal and medical queries. Our internal data, consistent with public SEMrush and SimilarWeb research, suggests something close to 85% of legal queries and 78% of medical-practice queries include a location modifier (a city, neighborhood, or *near me*). The user doesn't want a national result. They want a provider in their area. Google knows this. The algorithm has been heavily weighted toward local intent signals for over a decade, and it gets more localized every year.

That means competing for *lawyer in Florida* is a losing proposition for almost any single firm. You're up against national lawyer mills with billion-dollar budgets, mature SEO programs, and statewide brand equity. The math doesn't work. But competing for *probate lawyer Tampa* is tractable. You're up against five to fifteen other Tampa probate firms. The 3-pack has three spots. The first-page organic results have ten. With sustained operational discipline, ranking #1 against fifteen local competitors is winnable.

Implementation specifics for Hyperlocal Dominance:

**Per-metro keyword research.** We don't just identify primary city queries. We map neighborhoods, sub-markets, adjacent towns, and informal area names. Tampa includes Hyde Park, Westshore, Downtown, Channelside, South Tampa, North Tampa, Carrollwood, and so on. Each is a winnable sub-query if approached deliberately.

**Per-neighborhood landing pages where intent justifies them.** Not duplicate templated pages with neighborhood names swapped. Real pages with neighborhood-specific content, local hooks, neighborhood-specific FAQs, and intent-aligned conversion paths. If a Hyde Park resident searches *estate attorney Hyde Park*, the page they land on should feel like it was built for Hyde Park residents — not for Floridians.

**Per-metro Google Business Profile strategy.** Each metro gets its own GBP optimization plan with metro-specific categories, services taxonomy, photos, posts, and review velocity targets. For multi-location practices, each location is a coordinated node in the network rather than a parallel competitor.

**Per-metro authority signals.** Local press, local directories, local sponsorships, local bar associations, local chamber of commerce listings, neighborhood-specific community sites. Authority signals from within the metro carry more weight than generic national directories for local pack ranking.

**Per-metro content.** Tampa-specific Florida probate examples instead of generic probate articles. Florida-specific statutory references instead of national overviews. Neighborhood-specific intake stories instead of generic case studies (anonymized where appropriate).

The clearest example is AWS Law Firm. AWS doesn't rank for *probate lawyer Florida* — that's a different fight, and one we explicitly declined to engage. AWS ranks for *probate lawyer Tampa* and twenty related Tampa queries. Different fight. Winnable fight. Result: position one across the priority Tampa probate queries, with a moat that compounds every month.

## Pillar 2 — Data-First Reporting

Every Rysen engagement reports in revenue, citations, ranks, and dollars. Not impressions. Not reach. Not *campaign performance*. Every dollar of work we do gets tracked to a specific client outcome — a ranking gained, a citation earned, a phone call attributed, a revenue figure produced.

I wrote a longer piece on this in [The Death of the Impressions-Based Marketing Report](/blog/death-of-impressions-marketing-report) — go read that if you want the full argument. The short version: if you can't measure marketing in dollars, you can't optimize it. Most agencies report what they did. We report what it generated.

Implementation specifics:

**Live attribution dashboard.** Every client gets a real-time dashboard. It shows revenue by channel, engagement by source, attribution model results, ROI per channel, and trend data. The dashboard updates when new data arrives. The client can open it on their phone. No agency portal that requires logging in once a month — the dashboard is a live operational tool.

**Call tracking per channel.** We assign unique phone numbers to each channel using CallRail, WhatConverts, or equivalent. Organic search gets a number. Google Maps gets a different number. Paid search gets channel-specific numbers per campaign. Direct traffic gets a number. When a call comes in, we know exactly which channel produced it.

**UTM discipline.** Consistent source parameters applied to every paid touchpoint, every organic link we control, every email send. The discipline isn't glamorous — it's spreadsheet work. But without it, attribution erodes within months.

**CRM revenue feedback loop.** The firm's CRM (or their intake system) records the source of each engagement when it's signed. That source data flows back to the attribution model. The model then knows not just which channels produced leads, but which channels produced *signed engagements* — which is the only number that ultimately matters.

**Weighted attribution model with documented methodology.** We pick a model. We document why. For most legal firms with a 30-to-60-day sales cycle, we use a weighted model: first-touch 40%, last-touch 40%, middle touches share 20%. We document this in writing because the model's choice biases the results, and the client deserves to know which bias we've chosen.

**Weekly attribution review meeting.** Every Monday, we sit down with the partner and walk through the attribution data. What signed last week, where did it come from, what's the trend. The meeting is non-optional — it's the operational anchor that makes the dashboard meaningful instead of decorative.

**Monthly attribution report.** Written, narrative report with revenue by channel, year-to-date trend, ROI by channel, recommended budget adjustments, projected revenue impact. The report exists so the firm can defend marketing spend to the partnership or the executive team with specific data.

Concrete example: Tyler Family Law's attribution dashboard tracks revenue from organic search, local pack, AI citations, paid search, and direct traffic in real time. When a new engagement signs Tuesday afternoon, we know by Wednesday morning which channel and which query produced it. The partner can see exactly which channels produce her highest-value clients. She can make budget decisions with specific data — and she does.

## Pillar 3 — Compound Visibility

Rankings, reviews, citations, AI mentions, content authority — all of them reinforce each other. Each lever amplifies the others. We don't optimize one channel in isolation. We orchestrate all of them so they compound.

The argument: single-channel SEO peaks. Multi-channel orchestration compounds. A firm that ranks #1 in Google but is invisible in ChatGPT is leaving 25–40% of future-year leads on the table as AI search adoption climbs. A firm with two hundred reviews but page-two rankings loses every traditional search. A firm with great content but no authority citations gets ignored by AI training data. The surfaces only matter together.

The five surfaces we run for every engagement:

**1. Google organic search.** The traditional search results. Still the largest channel for most legal and medical queries, but declining as a share of total discovery. Foundation, not the whole game.

**2. Google Maps / local pack.** The 3-pack of map-based results that appears at the top of most local-intent queries. Often more valuable than organic position #1 because the 3-pack sits above organic results for queries with local intent.

**3. AI search.** ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Google AI Overviews. The fastest-growing discovery channel, and the one most firms are not yet optimizing for. Compounding cited authority here in 2024 is the equivalent of compounding Google authority in 2010 — early movers will be uncatchable in three years.

**4. Review platforms.** Google reviews, Avvo and Justia for legal, Healthgrades and Vitals for medical, specialty platforms (RealSelf for cosmetic dermatology, etc.). Review velocity is one of the most important local pack ranking signals and one of the highest-leverage trust signals for AI surfaces.

**5. Authority publications.** Legal directories with editorial weight (Martindale, Super Lawyers, Best Lawyers), trade press (Modern Law Practice, Search Engine Journal contributions), industry podcasts, local business journals. Authority citations strengthen entity signals across every other surface.

The implementation reality is that all five surfaces are interlocked. Better Google rankings lead to more organic referrals to the firm, which leads to more reviews when patients have a good experience, which strengthens local pack ranking, which produces more phone calls, which produces more signed engagements, which generates more authoritative case study content, which earns more authority citations, which strengthens AI training data weighting, which produces more AI citations, which produces more search volume, which feeds back to Google rankings. Each lever pulled in isolation does a fraction of what all five pulled together do. That's compound visibility.

Implementation specifics:

- Each surface gets a documented quarterly strategy with specific deliverables
- The strategies inform each other (the content portfolio is built to serve both Google ranking and AI citation; the review program is built to feed both local pack and Avvo authority signals)
- We monitor all five surfaces together in a single dashboard view
- We report on compound visibility, not individual channel performance
- Quarterly business reviews look at the whole portfolio, not channel-by-channel silos

Example: Hartman Dermatology. Six months into the engagement, all five surfaces moved together. Google rankings climbed. Local pack 3-pack appearances increased. Review velocity rose from 12-15 per month to 30+. AI citations went from zero meaningful presence to a 38% citation rate in priority Miami cosmetic dermatology queries. Authority placements landed on Healthgrades, RealSelf, and a Miami medical industry trade publication. The net result — cosmetic consultations up 78% — wasn't from one channel. It was from all five moving together.

## Pillar 4 — Weekly Accountability

Every Rysen client gets a standing weekly meeting. No exceptions. The meeting is the operational heartbeat of every engagement. We report what shipped, what's working, what we're adjusting, what's planned for the next thirty days. The dashboard gets reviewed together. The client gets to ask anything.

The argument: monthly reviews are where work disappears. The interval between monthly meetings is too long for honest accountability. An agency can produce a satisfying monthly report by frontloading work into the final week before the meeting, while letting the first three weeks drift. Quarterly reviews are even worse — they're where engagements quietly die. Weekly accountability surfaces problems early enough to fix them.

Implementation specifics:

**Standing weekly call.** Same day, same time, 45 to 60 minutes, on camera. The call has a documented agenda: what shipped last week, what we're working on this week, what's coming in the next 30 days, dashboard review, questions and decisions. The call doesn't get canceled. If the partner is unavailable on the regular day, we move the call — we don't skip it.

**Weekly written report.** Executive summary plus detail. The summary fits in a phone notification preview — three sentences on what mattered this week. The detail is the full report for anyone who wants to dig in. The written report serves two purposes: it forces us to put our work in writing every week, and it gives the partner a paper trail if they need to defend the engagement internally.

**Live dashboard reviewed together.** Not screenshots emailed afterward. Open the dashboard, walk through the numbers, make adjustments based on what we see. The dashboard is the source of truth.

**"Last week / this week / next 30 days" rolling cadence.** Every meeting covers all three windows. We report on what just happened, what's currently shipping, and what's planned for the next month. This rolling structure means there's never a gap between meetings where the partner doesn't know what's next.

**Direct Slack or text channel for the partner.** No agency portal. No ticketing system. The partner has the senior strategist's direct number. Urgent things get handled in real time. Non-urgent things get handled in the weekly meeting.

**Quarterly business review.** On top of the weekly tactical cadence. Quarterly meetings are 90 minutes, deep strategic, often with the broader firm leadership team present. We look at the prior quarter holistically, set the strategic frame for the next quarter, and review whether the engagement is producing what we promised.

Why most agencies don't do this: weekly accountability is expensive. It requires senior people on every account. It requires real preparation every week. It requires actually doing what you said you'd do. Most agencies prefer monthly reviews because they can hide a bad week inside a better month and a bad month inside a slightly better quarter. The weekly cadence makes hiding impossible — which is the point.

Example: every Rysen client knows what's happening every Friday morning. They have a written report in their inbox. They had a 45-minute meeting on Monday. They can text the senior strategist about anything at any time. Compare that to most agencies, where the firm has no clear idea what work was actually performed between monthly check-ins.

## Pillar 5 — Honest Selection

We turn down engagements. Not every firm is a fit for Rysen, and not every Rysen prospect is one we believe we can win for. We tell prospective clients no when the market is too saturated, when the timeline is too aggressive, when the expectations are unrealistic, or when our process won't generate what they need.

The argument: agencies that take every client to hit revenue targets dilute their playbook. They take clients they can't win for, which produces failed engagements, which hurts reputation, which hurts the existing clients they were succeeding with. The honest-selection discipline isn't moral — it's operational. Selective firms compound. Indiscriminate firms regress to the mean.

How we decide:

**Market saturation analysis.** Can we realistically reach #1 against the existing competitive set in this metro for this practice area? Some markets are dominated by incumbent firms with eight-year SEO programs and millions in cumulative authority signal. Beating them requires either a multi-year horizon or a specific angle the incumbent doesn't cover. If we can't articulate the path to #1, we say no.

**Practice area competitiveness.** Some practice areas are crowded with billion-dollar lawyer mills (personal injury in major metros, mass tort) where the economics of SEO compete against advertising budgets we can't match. We can sometimes win in those markets with surgical hyperlocal focus, but often the realistic answer is *not without spending more than your current revenue justifies*.

**Existing baseline.** Firms with strong foundations compound faster. A firm with a clean Google Business Profile, a reasonable review base, a coherent website, and competent partners moves quickly. A firm with profound foundational debt — broken site, no GBP, inconsistent naming, partnership conflicts about marketing direction — moves slowly regardless of agency competence.

**Founder or decision-maker buy-in.** No marketing engagement works without aligned leadership. If the managing partner is fully bought in and treats marketing as strategic, the engagement compounds. If marketing is a delegated annoyance to whoever has the least bandwidth that quarter, the engagement stalls regardless of how good the agency is. We screen for this carefully in discovery.

**Process fit.** Some firms want quarterly reviews and email updates. Our process is weekly meetings with active partner involvement. If a firm wants a passive engagement where the agency just *handles SEO* without their attention, we're not the right agency for them. There are agencies that do that well. We're not one of them.

Implementation specifics:

- 90-minute discovery call before any quote. The call covers history, goals, baseline, market, leadership buy-in, and process expectations.
- Independent analysis between discovery and quote. We don't quote off intuition. We look at the market, the firm's current position, and competitive landscape, and we form a view on whether we believe we can win.
- We tell roughly 30% of prospects we're not the right fit. Sometimes that's because of market saturation, sometimes because of practice-area competitiveness, sometimes because of leadership alignment, sometimes because process expectations don't match.
- We refer some prospects to other agencies we trust. The legal and medical marketing space has a few agencies we respect and refer to when we're not the right fit.
- We start engagements only when we're confident we can win. The confidence isn't a marketing claim — it's a real internal threshold.
- We end engagements when the relationship isn't producing. Rare, but it happens. Better for everyone than dragging out a stalled engagement.

Why this matters to clients: if Rysen takes you on, it's because we believe we can win for you. The selectivity is what compounds our portfolio — every successful engagement strengthens the case that we know how to pick the firms we engage. The discipline is uncomfortable on the revenue side (saying no to a willing prospect is hard), but it's the operational anchor that keeps our case studies real.
