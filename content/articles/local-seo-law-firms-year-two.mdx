I see the same pattern, over and over, in law firms that have been with an agency for two-plus years. Months one through eight are great. Rankings rise. Lead volume grows. By month eight or nine, the firm is ranked #1 or #2 for their priority queries. The managing partner is happy. The agency is happy. Everyone is doing the celebratory call.

Then year two starts. Quietly, things drift. The firm slips from #1 to #3 over six months. Lead volume softens. The agency, when asked, points to *competitive pressure* or *Google algorithm changes*. The managing partner starts wondering if SEO ever actually worked, or if they should be putting more money into paid search.

This is the most common failure pattern in law firm SEO. It is almost never about competition or Google. It is about operational discipline — specifically, the fact that the work that gets you to #1 is structurally different from the work that keeps you there, and most agencies don't shift operational gears between those phases. The firms that figure this out maintain dominance for years. The firms that don't oscillate between #1 and #5 forever, paying retainers for a result that quietly erodes.

I want to walk through why this happens, what year-two discipline actually looks like, how to tell if you're in a stall, and what a real year-two report should contain. None of this is theoretical — it's the operational model we run with every Rysen client when they cross the eight-month mark.

## The phase one versus phase two mistake

The work that gets a law firm from invisible to #1 in nine months is mostly *project work*. It has a defined scope. It produces measurable foundations. It can be billed against a project plan.

Specifically, phase one is:

- Schema markup overhaul (LegalService, Attorney, FAQPage, BreadcrumbList — full schema rebuild)
- Content gap filling (publish fifteen to twenty cornerstone practice-area articles)
- Citation building (get on Avvo, Justia, FindLaw, Martindale, the state bar's lawyer referral service, specialty-specific directories)
- Google Business Profile optimization sprint (category targeting, services taxonomy, photos, posts, attributes, completeness)
- Authority building sprint (legal directory profiles, podcast appearances, trade press placements)
- Technical SEO foundation (Core Web Vitals, mobile optimization, site architecture, internal linking, indexing)

Most of these are finite. Once they're done, they're done. You don't rebuild your schema every month. You don't re-claim your Avvo profile every month. The phase-one work creates the foundation that makes ranking possible. It compounds quickly because it's all upside — every piece of foundation work moves rankings.

The work that keeps a firm at #1 is structurally different. It is *operational work*, not project work. It doesn't have a defined end. It can't be billed against a project plan because it never finishes.

Specifically, phase two is:

- Review velocity discipline (consistent five to fifteen reviews per location per month, sustained for the duration of the engagement)
- Content refresh cadence (existing cornerstone articles updated quarterly with fresh examples, dates, and data — not abandoned)
- New competitor monitoring (every quarter, new firms enter your market; if you're not tracking them, you don't see the shift until it's too late)
- Schema and technical maintenance (Google updates schema requirements several times a year; your markup decays if it's not maintained)
- Authority signal compounding (you keep building citations and authority links, you never stop)
- Local pack optimization tuning (3-pack rankings shift weekly; the firm that monitors and adjusts holds position one; the firm that doesn't drifts)

Most agencies are good at phase one because phase one is measurable, billable, and rewarding. You can show the client a chart of the rankings climbing. The work feels productive. Most agencies are bad at phase two because phase two is operational discipline that's invisible until it's missing. You can't show the client a satisfying chart of *we did the maintenance work this month and nothing visibly changed*. The reward for phase-two work is the absence of decline.

That's a hard business model. It's why most agencies under-deliver on it.

## The five year-two failures

I'll name the five most common year-two failures explicitly. Each one is operational, each one is predictable, and each one is fixable if you catch it.

**The Content Graveyard.** Agencies stop publishing once rankings hit #1. The cornerstone articles published in phase one sit untouched. Six months later, the data is stale, the examples are dated, the competitive content has caught up and surpassed yours. Google rewards content freshness for evergreen queries — not as much as it rewards depth and authority, but enough that abandoning content quietly erodes rankings. Six months of stale content equals measurable drift. The fix: a documented content refresh schedule, quarterly review of every cornerstone article, refreshed examples and updated data on a published cadence.

**The Review Drought.** Review velocity drops below sustainable levels after the initial push. The firm goes from earning twelve reviews a month in phase one to four in phase two. Meanwhile, new competitors are running active review campaigns. The competitor with rising review velocity out-signals you for local pack ranking. The 3-pack changes. You lose the spot you held for nine months. The fix: review velocity targets per location, automated request flows, response handling protocol, weekly velocity tracking. Treat reviews like a metric, not like a side effect.

**The Competitive Blindspot.** The agency stops tracking new entrants. Three new firms enter your market over six months, each working hard on SEO. The agency, focused on maintaining what's already in place, doesn't notice. You find out when one of them shows up in the 3-pack and you don't, and by then they have nine months of foundation work that you have to overtake. The fix: quarterly competitive landscape audits with documented changes — new firms in market, new content from competitors, new citation patterns. If your agency can't produce a recent competitive landscape document on request, they're not doing this work.

**The Schema Decay.** Google updates schema markup requirements several times a year — deprecating fields, adding new ones, tightening enforcement of properties that were previously optional. Your phase-one schema gets out of date. Some of your markup starts triggering Rich Results errors. AI search engines stop citing you because your entity signals are unclear. The decline is invisible until you check Search Console. The fix: quarterly schema audits, alignment with current best practices, fixing flagged issues within weeks rather than months.

**The Single-Touch Mindset.** The agency reports *you're still #1* every month without acknowledging that #1 in year one is a different problem from #1 in year two. They missed the shift. They keep doing phase-one tactics, billing for phase-one work, and producing diminishing returns. The fix: an explicit transition conversation around month six to eight, with a documented phase-two operating model that the agency commits to running. If your agency can't articulate what changes between phase one and phase two, they don't know.

## What year-two discipline actually looks like

I'll be specific. Here is the operational cadence we run for every Rysen client past month eight. This isn't theoretical — it's the operating manual. Adjust the specifics to your context, but the structure should look something like this.

**Weekly:**
- Rank tracking review across Google and AI search surfaces
- Review velocity report per location, against target
- GMB posts and photo updates executed on schedule
- New competitor scan (any new firms in tracked queries this week)
- Citation maintenance check (broken links, NAP changes, unflagged updates)
- Standing client meeting with the partner — 45 to 60 minutes, same day every week, on camera

**Monthly:**
- Two to four new authority articles published, under named attorney bylines
- Four to six existing cornerstone articles refreshed (data updated, examples updated, internal links audited)
- Schema audit and any necessary updates
- Local pack 3-pack analysis with photo evidence of placement changes
- Authority outreach campaign — one to two new authoritative citations earned
- Written monthly attribution report covering revenue by channel, content velocity, citation velocity, rank delta, AI citation rate

**Quarterly:**
- Full competitive landscape audit with documented changes since last quarter
- Strategic refresh meeting — what's working, what's not, what we're adjusting
- Content gap re-analysis (have we covered the practice-area topics that have emerged this quarter?)
- Technical SEO health check (Core Web Vitals, mobile, structured data, indexing)
- AI search visibility audit across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Claude
- Authority signal review (what's accumulating, what's stalled)

**Annually:**
- Full content portfolio review (refresh, retire, expand)
- Schema overhaul aligned with current best practices
- Local strategy refresh — new neighborhoods, adjacent practice areas, expansion opportunities
- New market evaluation if the firm is considering growth

Notice the cadence: weekly, monthly, quarterly, annually. Each tier has specific deliverables. None of this is *we'll get to it when we have time*. It's scheduled. It's reportable. It's accountable.

Most agencies don't run this cadence because it's expensive to staff. It requires senior people, weekly preparation, real discipline. It's much cheaper to run a monthly check-in and call it phase-two work. That's why most engagements stall. The phase-two operating cost is roughly twice the phase-one cost per month, and most agencies either don't charge for it or can't deliver against it.

## How to tell if you're in a year-two stall

There are eight signals to watch for. If you see three or more, you're probably in a discipline gap, not a competitive squeeze.

1. Rank drift from #1 to #3 over three or more months on priority queries
2. Review velocity below five per month per location for sixty-plus days
3. No new authority articles published in sixty-plus days
4. Existing cornerstone articles not refreshed in six-plus months
5. No new citations earned in a full quarter
6. GMB engagement metrics (calls, direction requests, profile views) flat or declining month over month
7. Local pack appearances decreasing in your priority neighborhoods
8. New competitor names appearing in your tracked rankings that your agency hasn't flagged

If you see three or more of these in your current data, you're in a stall. The agency may be telling you everything is fine. The data isn't.

The fix isn't necessarily firing the agency. Sometimes the fix is bringing this list to the agency, demanding a phase-two operating plan, and giving them sixty days to implement it. If they can deliver, the relationship is repaired and the rankings recover. If they can't, you have a clean reason to make a change.

## What real year-two reports look like

A real year-two monthly report contains specific data. Not vibes, not narrative, not *we continued our SEO work this month*. Specific data.

**Sample year-two monthly report contents:**

- Current rank for all priority queries, with delta versus last month and versus three months ago
- Review velocity per location, with target versus actual, and the trailing 90-day average
- Content published this month — count, topics, byline authors, internal linking applied
- Content refreshed this month — count, articles, specific updates made, reasons for the refresh
- Citations earned this month — count, sources, type (directory, publication, podcast, etc.)
- AI search visibility — citation count and queries across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews
- Local pack appearances per metro, with photo evidence of placement
- Revenue attribution by channel for the month
- Specific plan for next month — articles to publish, articles to refresh, citations to pursue, technical fixes to ship

If your year-two report is the same template as your year-one report, you're not getting year-two work. If your year-two report is shorter or vaguer than your year-one report, you're getting less value than you got in year one. If your year-two report doesn't have specific revenue attribution per channel, you're getting impression-report theater dressed up as SEO maintenance.
