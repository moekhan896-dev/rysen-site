A managing partner I know recently forwarded me his Q3 marketing report. Eight point four million impressions. Two hundred forty-seven thousand website visits. Twelve hundred leads. Three charts, four call-out boxes, a quote from his account manager about *strong momentum heading into Q4*.

I asked him one question: how many of those twelve hundred leads became clients? He didn't know. The report didn't say. He'd been paying this agency $14,000 a month for three years, and his quarterly report could not answer the question *did you produce any revenue this quarter*.

This is the dominant marketing reporting pattern at professional services firms in 2024. It is also indefensible. The age of the impressions-based marketing report is ending, and the partners and CMOs who continue accepting them are choosing ignorance over accountability. I want to walk through why these reports exist, why they survive, what real attribution looks like, and the four questions every CMO should be asking the agency that sent the last report to their inbox.

## Why impressions reports exist

Some history. Pre-2010, attribution was genuinely hard. Tracking pixels were primitive. Cookies were limited and unreliable. CRMs didn't connect well to marketing tools. Multi-touch attribution required custom integration work that most agencies couldn't justify. So the industry standardized around what could be measured: impressions, reach, frequency, click-through rate, sessions, time on site. These were available, they were repeatable, and they let agencies show clients *something* in a monthly report.

Then a useful workaround calcified into an entire reporting paradigm. By 2015, the technology to do real attribution existed — call tracking platforms were mature, CRMs had decent marketing integration, weighted attribution models were a known science. By 2020 there was no defensible technological reason a professional services agency couldn't track revenue. By 2024 the gap is unambiguous. The reason most agencies still report in impressions isn't technology. It's incentive.

A 4.2-million impressions number sounds impressive. A *$0 in attributed revenue this quarter* number does not. An agency that reports in impressions can produce a satisfying report even when the campaign generated nothing. An agency that reports in revenue cannot. Impressions reports survive because they make agencies look productive regardless of whether they actually produced anything. The CMO sees big numbers. The agency keeps the retainer. Both parties have an unspoken agreement not to ask the question that would expose what the work actually generated.

This is a fragile equilibrium and it's collapsing. The CMOs and partners who push through it ahead of their peers gain a real competitive advantage. The ones who don't are committing to making marketing decisions with no actual data.

## The attribution pyramid

Most marketing metrics fall into one of four tiers. I call this the Attribution Pyramid because each tier sits on top of the last, and each tier is more expensive to produce and more useful for decision-making than the one below it.

**Tier 1 — Exposure metrics.** Impressions, reach, ad views, brand mentions. These measure whether your marketing was *visible* — whether someone could have noticed it. They are the cheapest metrics to produce, the least correlated with revenue, and the easiest to game. An agency can inflate a 4-million-impression number by buying cheap programmatic display inventory that no one actually sees. The impression count goes up. Nothing else changes.

**Tier 2 — Engagement metrics.** Clicks, sessions, page views, time on site, bounce rate, scroll depth. These measure whether someone *interacted* with your marketing. They're better than exposure metrics because they require a human action. But they're still proxy. A high click-through rate on an ad doesn't mean revenue. A long time on site doesn't mean a signed engagement. Engagement metrics are useful for optimizing creative and channel mix, but they shouldn't be the headline number on a quarterly report.

**Tier 3 — Intent metrics.** Form fills, calls, demo requests, signed contact requests, quote requests. These are closer to revenue because they require the prospect to take an action that signals real intent. A consultation request is a meaningful signal. A free audit signup is a meaningful signal. But Tier 3 metrics still aren't revenue — many leads don't close, the leads that do close vary wildly in value, and the lead count alone doesn't tell you which marketing tactics produced the *valuable* leads versus the time-wasters.

**Tier 4 — Revenue attribution.** Actual dollars produced, by source, by channel, with a documented audit trail. This is the only tier that answers the question every partner actually cares about: *what did my marketing spend generate?* Tier 4 requires call tracking per channel, UTM discipline across every touchpoint, a CRM that records the source of each engagement, a defined attribution model (first-touch, last-touch, or weighted), and a feedback loop where signed engagement revenue flows back to the marketing system.

The whole pyramid is fine to look at. Tier 1 through 3 metrics are useful inputs for tactical optimization. But Tier 1 through 3 metrics *in the absence of Tier 4* are a red flag. If your agency reports primarily in Tiers 1 and 2, ask why they don't report in Tier 4. The answer reveals the agency.

## What real attribution actually looks like

Here's a concrete example. One of our clients — an Atlanta family law firm I'll call Firm A — gets a monthly attribution report that looks roughly like this for Q4 2024:

**By channel:**
- $87,400 attributed revenue from Google organic search
- $34,200 attributed revenue from Google Maps / local pack
- $22,800 attributed revenue from ChatGPT citations
- $8,400 attributed revenue from Perplexity citations
- $4,200 attributed revenue from organic referral traffic
- **Total attributed revenue: $157,000**

Each line item ties back to specific signed engagements. Each engagement has a documented source URL, query (where the query was captured), date of first contact, first call timestamp, signed engagement date, and fee amount. The attribution model is documented (weighted, with first-touch credited at 40%, last-touch at 40%, and middle touches sharing 20% — chosen because the firm's average sales cycle is 45 days and the model fits empirically).

The managing partner of Firm A can open the live attribution dashboard right now, on his phone, and see which channels produced which engagements signed last week. He can ask *if we cut the local SEO budget in half, what's the projected revenue impact?* — and we can answer with a model based on actual data, not vibes. He can ask *which practice areas are getting the highest-value leads?* — and the dashboard breaks it out. He can ask *what would it cost to add another $50K in monthly attributed revenue?* — and we have a defensible answer.

That's what real marketing attribution looks like at a professional services firm. It is achievable. It is operationally expensive but technologically straightforward. And every firm we engage with gets it, because we built the firm around the discipline of producing it.

The infrastructure required: call tracking with unique numbers per channel (CallRail, WhatConverts, or similar — under $200/month for most firms), source URL parameters applied consistently across every paid and organic touchpoint, a CRM with marketing-source fields that actually get filled in (not optional — the firm has to commit to the data discipline), a weighted attribution model documented in writing, a weekly review cadence where the dashboard gets looked at together. None of this is exotic. All of it is operational rigor that most agencies don't want to commit to.

## The four questions every CMO should demand

If you're a CMO or managing partner at a professional services firm, ask your current agency these four questions this week. Don't accept hand-waving or *we're working on building that out* answers. Demand specifics.

**Question 1: Can you show me the dollar amount of revenue attributed to your work this quarter? Not pipeline. Not estimated lifetime value. Revenue.** If the answer is anything other than a specific number with a dollar sign and a documented methodology, you have an attribution problem.

**Question 2: By which specific channel did that revenue come?** Not *paid social*. Not *SEO*. Which specific channel — Google organic, Google Maps, paid search by campaign, LinkedIn, ChatGPT citations, Perplexity citations, organic referral, email marketing. Each line item with a dollar amount attached. If the agency can't break it out at this level, they can't optimize at this level — which means they're guessing.

**Question 3: What's your weighted attribution model and why did you choose it?** This is the question that separates real attribution from theater. There are four common models — first-touch, last-touch, linear, time-decay — and each one biases the results in different ways. A serious agency will have picked one, documented the choice, and have a defensible reason. An agency that hasn't is reporting whatever number looks best at the time.

**Question 4: If we cut your budget in half tomorrow, which line items would you cut and what would the revenue impact be?** This is the question most agencies dread. It forces them to admit which of their activities they believe matter and which they're billing for out of habit. A serious agency can answer this in real time with specific projections. An agency that can't is billing you for activity, not outcomes.

If your agency can't answer these four questions in 24 hours with specific numbers, you don't have a measurement problem. You have an agency problem. You wouldn't accept *we'll get back to you on that* from your CFO. Don't accept it from your marketing agency.

## Why agencies actively resist this

Let me be direct about why most agencies don't want to do real attribution. It exposes which of their tactics produce nothing — which is often a lot of them. It removes the option to inflate the value of work with vanity metrics. It shifts the conversation from *look at all this activity* to *look at the dollars*. It makes them accountable for outcomes instead of effort. It enables clients to fire them for specific reasons, which is much easier than the current dynamic where the client suspects the agency isn't producing but can't quite prove it.

The *we can't track that* excuse is almost never true in 2024. The technology is mature. The integrations are documented. The methodology is well-understood. When an agency says they can't track revenue attribution, what they're saying is that they don't want to be measured that way. That's a polite refusal, not a technical limitation.

Some agencies will say they do attribution but actually only do Tier 3 metrics — they report on form fills and call counts as if those were revenue. They're not. A lead count is not a revenue number. Pipeline is not revenue. *Marketing-qualified leads* is not revenue. If the column header on your report doesn't have a dollar sign in it, it's not revenue attribution.

The agencies that built their operating model around impression reporting are going to fight this transition hard. They have to — their entire margin structure depends on clients not asking the right questions. The transition will play out the same way most market transitions play out: a small minority of agencies will get ahead of it, the majority will resist, and over five years the resistance will erode as the firms that demand attribution force the industry to provide it.
